Methodology

Evaluating LLM-generated survival explanations

Question

When a language model explains a pancreatic cancer survival prediction, can its stated confidence be trusted, and does that depend on how it is prompted?

Approach

I generated 300 synthetic pancreatic cancer survival cases and selected 50 by stratified sampling. MedGemma (via Ollama) explained each case under eight prompt and decoding set-ups, giving 400 explanations. A fully automated framework scored each one on numerical correctness, risk-label agreement, robustness, and faithfulness.

Findings

  • How credible a confidence signal is depends on the prompt style.
  • Misplaced certainty poses a greater clinical safety risk than hallucination.

Why it matters

Before LLMs explain risk to clinicians or patients, each confidence signal needs its own calibration requirements. Limits: synthetic patients, one model and one cancer type. I have since extended the idea to multiple models in a separate paper, now under review.

Methodology

MedOntoKG

Question

Thousands of new abstracts on Alzheimer's and other neurodegenerative diseases appear every year. Can NLP turn that literature into a structured, navigable map of what is known about diagnosis, prognosis and treatment?

Approach

I collected 23,897 Scopus abstracts (2022–2024) and cleaned them to 17,851. A pretrained biomedical tagger labelled candidate terms, and I fine-tuned XLNet to assign them to 41 entity types. A second XLNet model classified each abstract as diagnosis, prognosis or treatment. Relations between terms came from cosine similarity of fine-tuned XLNet embeddings (threshold 0.90), and the results were organised into an RDF ontology per aspect and a Neo4j knowledge graph.

Findings

  • Abstract classification reached 85% validation accuracy (weighted F1 0.85) on 3,571 held-out abstracts. It was weakest on prognosis, the smallest class.
  • Entity typing reached F1 0.69 across 41 types, with early stopping to limit overfitting.
  • The ontologies capture sub-classes such as signs and symptoms, lab values, severity, medication and therapeutic procedures.

Why it matters

An ontology-backed graph makes the evidence behind each answer traceable, a foundation for knowledge-driven decision support. Limits: entity labels came from a pretrained tagger rather than expert annotation, the classes were imbalanced, and some extracted terms are still noisy.

Methodology

Hybrid ontology extraction

Question

Manual ontology building is precise but slow; fully automated LLM extraction scales but drifts. How should the work be split between human annotators and an LLM to get both quality and scale?

Approach

We split ten papers on hybrid intelligence: five were annotated manually, and five went straight to the LLM for triple generation. Direct generation produced heavily repetitive output, so we redesigned it as a hybrid. The manual ontology now defines the classes, GPT-4o Turbo extracts entities within them with a confidence score per term, and relations are matched using FastText embeddings and cosine similarity. The result was linked to DBpedia and queried with SPARQL.

Findings

  • Direct LLM generation produced many repeated triples, plus contradictions and out-of-scope relations.
  • Ontology-guided prompts, confidence intervals and similarity thresholds reduced these issues (qualitative comparison).
  • The ontology grew from 114 to 811 triples.

Why it matters

LLMs work best as co-pilots for knowledge engineering, with humans owning the structure. Limits: a small corpus and a qualitative comparison; a link-prediction test (ComplEx) scored low (MRR 0.05), reflecting how sparse the graph still is.

My role

I worked primarily on the LLM triple generation: the first direct-generation attempt, and the redesign that guided GPT-4o Turbo with the manual ontology to extract entities and propose the relations that became new triples.
